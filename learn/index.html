TOOLS OVERVIEW

Match the tool to the job

Use distress tolerance when the immediate goal is to get through a high-intensity moment without making it worse. Use mindfulness to notice clearly. Use emotional regulation and CBT when you have enough space to examine patterns. Use interpersonal skills for requests, boundaries, and relationships. Use wellness, goal setting, and recreativity to support the conditions that make all the other skills easier to use.

Not sure where to start? Use Skills Finder to turn how you are feeling into a few relevant starting points.

01

GOAL SETTING

Make the next action easier to see

Good goals connect what matters to behavior that is small, specific, and reviewable.

Start smaller

Tiny next step

Use when: the goal feels too large, vague, or easy to avoid.

  1. Name the outcome you care about.
  2. Ask, “What action could I complete in 2 to 10 minutes?”
  3. Make the action visible and concrete, such as opening the document, putting shoes by the door, or writing one sentence.
  4. After completing it, choose the next small action rather than planning the whole project.
Cue the behavior

If-then plan

Use when: you know what you want to do but forget, delay, or rely on motivation.

Complete the sentence: If [specific situation happens], then I will [specific action].

Example: “If I finish breakfast, then I will spend five minutes reviewing my priorities.”

Direction

Values to action

Use when: you are busy but unsure whether your effort is moving toward what matters.

  1. Choose one value, such as learning, connection, health, creativity, or reliability.
  2. Name one behavior that would express that value this week.
  3. Choose the smallest version you can schedule.
Recovery

Reset after a missed day

Use when: one missed action turns into “I have failed” or “I will restart later.”

Review without punishment: What got in the way? What part of the plan was unrealistic? What smaller or better-timed action would make the next attempt easier?

02

DBT · DISTRESS TOLERANCE

Get through the moment without adding new problems

These skills are for short-term stabilization when emotion, urges, or stress are too intense for detailed problem-solving.

Pause

STOP skill

Use when: you feel pulled to react immediately.

  1. Stop: do not act on the first impulse.
  2. Take a step back: create physical or mental space.
  3. Observe: notice thoughts, feelings, body sensations, urges, and facts.
  4. Proceed mindfully: choose the next action that best serves your longer-term goal.
Ground

Five-senses grounding

Use when: you are spiraling, panicky, numb, or disconnected from the present.

Slowly identify five things you can see, four you can feel, three you can hear, two you can smell, and one you can taste. Accuracy is not the goal, attention is.

Lower activation

Paced breathing and body reset

Use when: your body feels highly activated.

Try a slower exhale than inhale for one to three minutes. Add brief, safe movement or cool water on the face if that feels appropriate for your body.

Avoid extreme temperature or exercise strategies if they are not medically safe for you. Use a gentler breathing or grounding practice instead.
Ride it out

Urge surfing

Use when: an urge feels intense but you do not want to act on it.

Rate the urge from 0 to 10. Notice where it shows up in the body. Watch it rise, peak, and shift like a wave. Re-rate every few minutes while choosing a safe alternative action.

03

DBT · MINDFULNESS

Notice what is happening, then return on purpose

Mindfulness is not about emptying the mind. It is practice in noticing attention, experience, and choice.

What skills

Observe, describe, participate

  1. Observe: notice sensations, thoughts, emotions, and events.
  2. Describe: use simple factual words, such as “my shoulders are tense” instead of “everything is terrible.”
  3. Participate: enter the activity you chose, one moment at a time.
Integration

Wise mind pause

Use when: emotion and logic seem to be pulling in opposite directions.

Ask three questions: What is emotion telling me? What do the observable facts suggest? What choice respects both the facts and what matters to me?

How skills

One-mindful and nonjudgmental

Do one thing at a time and describe it without adding labels of good, bad, weak, or stupid. When attention wanders, notice that and return.

Daily practice

Three-minute noticing

Spend one minute noticing thoughts, one minute noticing body sensations, and one minute widening attention to the whole environment. Finish by naming the next intentional action.

04

DBT · INTERPERSONAL EFFECTIVENESS

Ask clearly, protect connection, keep self-respect

Relationship skills work best when you know which goal matters most in the moment: getting an objective, protecting the relationship, or protecting self-respect.

Get an objective

DEAR MAN

  1. Describe the situation factually.
  2. Express feelings or opinions.
  3. Assert the request or boundary clearly.
  4. Reinforce why cooperation helps.
  5. Mindful: return to the point.
  6. Appear confident: use steady language and posture.
  7. Negotiate: look for workable alternatives.
Protect the relationship

GIVE

Gentle: avoid attacks. Interested: listen. Validate: show that you understand the other person’s perspective. Easy manner: reduce unnecessary intensity when appropriate.

Protect self-respect

FAST

Fair: be fair to yourself and the other person. Apologies: do not over-apologize for existing or having needs. Stick to values: keep your priorities in view. Truthful: avoid exaggerating or making excuses.

Before the conversation

Choose your priority

Write down three possible goals: what you want, how you want the relationship to feel afterward, and what you need to respect about yourself. Rank them before you start the conversation.

05

WELLNESS

Support the conditions that make skills easier

Wellness is not a perfect routine. It is a regular check on basic needs and patterns that can raise or lower vulnerability to stress.

Baseline

Wellness basics check

Ask what is most relevant today: sleep, regular food and hydration, movement, prescribed medication routines, substance use, pain or illness, time outdoors, and supportive contact with other people.

Choose one realistic adjustment, not a complete lifestyle overhaul.

Energy

Minimum viable routine

Choose a morning anchor, a midday anchor, and an evening anchor. Keep each one small enough to do on a difficult day, such as opening the blinds, eating something, or preparing tomorrow’s first task.

Connection

Connection plan

List three levels of contact: low effort, medium effort, and deeper support. Examples might include sending a message, sharing a walk, or asking someone for a conversation.

Review

Weekly energy audit

Make two columns: “usually gives energy” and “usually costs energy.” Look for one thing to protect and one thing to reduce, delegate, shorten, or recover from.

06

DBT · EMOTIONAL REGULATION

Understand the emotion before choosing the response

Emotions provide information, but the first action urge is not always the action that fits the facts or your goals.

Clarify

Check the facts

  1. Name the emotion and its intensity.
  2. Describe the prompting event without assumptions.
  3. List the interpretations your mind added.
  4. Ask what evidence supports or challenges those interpretations.
  5. Re-rate the emotion after considering the fuller picture.
Change action

Opposite action

Use when: the emotion does not fit the facts, or acting on the urge would move you away from your goals.

Identify the urge, then choose a behavior that goes in the opposite direction. If sadness urges withdrawal, a small opposite action might be getting out of bed, opening the curtains, or contacting someone.

Name it

Emotion map

Write down: prompting event, body sensations, thoughts, action urge, action taken, and what happened afterward. Patterns become easier to change when they are visible.

Build positives

Short-term and long-term positives

Schedule one small pleasant activity for today and one meaningful activity that supports a longer-term value. Both matter.

07

RECREATIVITY

Use play and creativity as restorative practice

Recreativity is a wellness-oriented space for low-pressure curiosity, play, making, and novelty. It is not a formal psychotherapy model.

Two minutes

Creative reset

Set a two-minute timer and make something with no performance goal: doodle shapes, arrange objects, hum a melody, write six unusual words, or take one interesting photograph.

The point is shifting from evaluation to curiosity.

Choice menu

Recreation menu

Create three lists: activities under 5 minutes, under 30 minutes, and longer than an hour. Include options for low, medium, and high energy days.

Novelty

One small new thing

Try a safe, low-cost variation: a different walking route, a new recipe, a new drawing tool, a library section you never visit, or a different playlist.

Permission

Play without productivity

Choose an activity that does not need to become a project, achievement, side hustle, or measurable improvement. Notice what happens when enjoyment is allowed to be enough.

08

CBT SKILLS

Test thoughts and change behavior patterns

CBT skills look at the interaction between situations, thoughts, emotions, body sensations, and behavior.

Thoughts

Thought record

  1. Describe the situation.
  2. Name the strongest emotion and rate it 0 to 100.
  3. Write the automatic thought.
  4. List evidence that supports the thought and evidence that does not.
  5. Write a more balanced alternative thought.
  6. Re-rate the emotion.
Behavior

Behavioral activation

Use when: low mood or avoidance is shrinking your routine.

Pick one small activity connected to mastery, pleasure, connection, or self-care. Put it at a specific time. Afterward, record what you predicted you would feel and what you actually felt.

Thinking patterns

Spot the thinking habit

Look for common patterns such as all-or-nothing thinking, mind reading, catastrophizing, overgeneralizing, discounting positives, and treating feelings as proof. Naming a pattern is a prompt to investigate, not proof that the thought is false.

Experiment

Test a prediction

Turn a belief into something observable. Write your prediction, choose a safe test, decide what evidence would count, run the test, then update the belief based on what happened.

IMPORTANT

Use the right level of support

These lessons are educational. If distress is persistent, worsening, affecting daily functioning, or hard to manage safely, consider working with a qualified mental health professional. If you are in immediate danger or may act on thoughts of suicide or self-harm, contact local emergency services or go to the nearest emergency department.
